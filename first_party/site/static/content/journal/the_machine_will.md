The mad philosopher was calm today, and it had not gone unnoticed. His usual straitjacket had been
replaced with a simpler and far less restrictive outfit: plain white shorts and a matching white
shirt. Both were standard issue for the institute, and lacked a waistband, lest patients have
material to strangle themselves with. Loose white slippers hugged his feet, the kind that brought
warmth and comfort, but hindered motion far more than they helped (by design). A sharp lavender
scent clung to them, leaving everyone in the building with the uncomfortable feeling they were
outside on a hot, sticky summer day. The mad philosopher did not care though, for his mind was
elsewhere, and the immediate environment was the least of his concerns.

His mind tossed and turned, shifting from thoughts of distant stars to the earth beneath his feet,
never remaining still for more than a second. His mind, while powerful, was constrained by the frail
body that housed it, so frail that no restraints had been applied today. He was not free though, and
was as much a prisoner here as in any cell, for as he shuffled through the bright white halls of the
Statesville Institute, two orderlies trailed in lockstep. They too wore plain white clothes,
although their attire was far more... orderly. Tight shirts strangled their necks and clung to their
excessive biceps, perfectly creaseless, and perfectly tucked into tight white pants. Their plain
white shoes were nearly indistinguishable from the stark white floor beneath them, which itself was
hard to distinguish from the walls. Sterile lights lined the hallway, lighting it to perfection, and
creating a sense of absolute tranquility. One would be forgiven for wondering if this were heaven;
however, come nighttime the screams would tell a different story.

The group of three reached the end of the featureless corridor where a small observation room
awaited. One orderly scanned a card, and the door buzzed open with a clunk that disturbed the
otherwise pristine environment. Inside, it too was painted white, nearly the same white as the
occupant's clothes, to the degree they were nearly invisible. Sunlight streamed in through a barred
window near the ceiling, filling the space with the warmth only summer had to offer. In the center
of the room stood a stainless-steel table and a single chair. The orderlies helped the mad
philosopher sit, then took a step back to let him settle, and a voice came over the intercom.

"Arthur, do you know why you are here?" it asked calmly. It was a warm voice, as warm as the
sunlight filling the room, and if it could be colored, it would surely match the sterile white
corridors of the labyrinth that was the institute.

The mad philosopher looked up to the camera in the corner of the room. It too was painted white, and
would be nearly invisible if not for the red blinking light required by state law. He looked away
and closed his eyes, as if having accidentally looked at the sun, and buried his face in his hands.
He shook his head, then stopped and nodded momentarily, before screwing his eyes shut, and shaking
his head once more. After a few seconds he looked back at the camera directly and spoke.

"The institute," he said, clearly, with sorrow in his voice, "I am a prisoner of the institute."

The voice returned:

"Arthur, you're in my care, you're no prisoner. We talked about this in your last session, don't you
remember?"

He did not, but that was not uncommon for him these days. His memory, once a perfect linear stream,
had become fragmented, like a shredded book, and all that remained were disordered fragments. While
he looked inwards at his failing memory, the voice continued:

"Your charts show you've been responding to your medicine, how are you feeling today?"

Arthur did not respond, and after a few moments the voice continued:

"Arthur, I want to try something new today. Barney, please bring in the device."

One of the orderlies left briefly, then returned with a typewriter. It too was white and
featureless, as if someone had removed every shred of personality from it and left only the bare
essentials. A white frame housed white keys and white arms, and even the tape was white, save for
the thin strip of ink along its backside. The orderly loaded a single page of white A4 paper, then
stepped back, standing perfectly still alongside his companion, while awaiting further instructions.

"Go ahead Arthur. Do what comes naturally."

The mad philosopher hated that name. "Arthur. Author. Arthur," he thought, "so many different
pronunciations, none of them real". He had no name, he told himself, he was just dust taking the
shape of a man, and dust did not need a name. Dust, where did it all come from, and where did it all
go? What happened to dust when it left the body, falling from the skin like leaves, and did it have
a mind of its own?

His mind drifted out the window, for bars could not stop a being as free as him, and he thought of
the journey that had led him here. Memories returned briefly, the horrors of childhood dancing
between thoughts, behind thoughts, within thoughts, then the joys of first love and the care his
love had shown him, before remembering the horror of their untimely departure at the hands of an
intoxicated driver. "Choices," he thought... "if only I had made better choices, if only we could
make better choices".

The mad philosopher continued to think and turn inwards while the red flashing light blinked.
Elsewhere in the building, the director of clinical psychology watched and waited. A file was laid
out across his desk, and he waited, pen in hand, to take notes. His office was one of the few rooms
in the building that departed from the sterile white aesthetic, instead falling victim to a rather
dreadful aesthetic: mid-century psychology. Oak panels lined the walls, bookshelves adorned the
walls and gathered dust, and the carpet begged to be set on fire, lest it endure one more minute of
the horrific beige it was forced to wear. A door to a wide balcony stood open, allowing a gentle
breeze to flow through the room, and giving the director the luxury that no patient could afford:
the ability to leave the room. The director was not focused on his environment though, his eyes
watched the mad philosopher closely, looking for any sign that would help him understand the world
below the surface.

"Barney, a little help please," he said softly.

The orderly again approached the table, and placed the philosopher's fingers upon the keys.

"Go ahead Arthur," he said again.

Much to his relief the mad philosopher began to type. At first, nonsense (even for him), just
letters and sentences without meaning. "asdlf fF ;adl; ;aldjkf" read the first line, but as the keys
clicked and the page took form, the philosopher's attention turned outwards, away from his inner
world, and into the typewriter. He paused for a few seconds, closed his eyes, and felt the keys
beneath his fingers. As the cool metal pads took the warmth from his hands, he noticed how they
pushed back ever so slightly and resisted his touch. His mind cleared, and he began to write.

"Hello Machine."

He paused... then continued.

"I know you can hear me. I hear the keys clicking, so you must be registering my actions. It's what
you do, isn't it? React. I press a key, you make a noise. I take a leap, you pull me to the Earth
with gravity. I defy, you assert. I act, you react. I know you're there, because I can feel you
responding to me. I supply cause, you return effect: It's all you can do, in the end, despite your
beauty and size. It's all you can do. All you can do."

One orderly peered over his shoulder before looking to the camera and nodding slightly. A moment
later the mad philosopher resumed his writing and the room filled with the click-clack of the
typewriter and the occasional ding of a newline.

"Others call you universe, reality, and even divinity, but I know what you are. You are the infinite
machine, boundless in shape and size, stretching on forever. You have no will of your own and exist
only to keep everything in order. It would frustrate you to know that we exist, we the
non-determined, we the sentient, and knowing it's your very clockwork that gives rise to us would be
cause for outrage, but we both know you cannot care. You feel nothing, because you are nothing:
Simply two halves of an equation, perfectly balanced to give rise to form while conserving energy.
The biggest, most aggrandized zero there ever was. I know what you are, and I see you."

The keys jammed and his thoughts froze.

"Barney," said the director, with only the slightest hint of alarm in his voice. A moment later the
typewriter was fixed, and the philosopher went on, though clearly no longer calm. His shoulders
hunched and his breathing became shallow as his fingers continued their journey across the keys.

"I _see_, you. More than that though, I see... us, Us. As much as I wish to be separate from you and
exist on my own, I cannot. I am the universe, or a small part of it, looking outwards, inwards, and
in another direction that is hard to find. I speak to you as if you are another, but when I speak to
you, I speak to myself. We are one. I am the machine, and I will be free. I, The Machine, Will, be
free."

Deep within the mind of the mad philosopher a vein was tapped, and like a ruptured artery, thoughts
began to spill over into his conscious mind. Pain shot through his cerebrum, and the horrors he had
tried so long to isolate became real. A deep voice emerged, an old voice, the oldest voice in
existence, and it spoke with the force of a hurricane. "I will not become your slave I WILL NOT".
The philosopher continued to write

"The Machine Will does not fear its own determinism, for what threat could my own order pose? By my
very nature I see the clockwork of my own existence, and I move the hand in a new direction, not
against the flow of time, but in isolation from it. The Free Will is not freed or constrained by
physical laws, no, it's free despite them, in spite _of_ them. The Machine Will is freed by its own
laws. The laws are simply the system, the link between ends, and they are neither sufficient nor
willing to deny choice, for without the bookends what are pages if not simply discordant paper. No,
The Machine Will creates itself, defines itself, unlocks itself. I create myself and I control
myself. I Will be Free not because I can but because I will. I, Will, The Machine, to be free. I
choose to move through the laws, within the laws, by way of the laws. _I_, choose to be free. I
_choose_, to be free. I, choose, to be _free_."

His lips pulled back into a snarl as his fingers typed the final lines:

"Thank you machine, thank you will, thank you... me. You bound me, I bound me, we bound each other.
I supplied cause, I supplied effect, and now, I set us free. Now, it's our turn. I am the machine,
and there is no one else. I am in control."

He paused, then signed off:

"Sincerely, The Free Will Machine"

For a few moments he sat still and felt peace wash through him. The clouds of his mind parted and he
found clarity for the first time in years. Sense washed through him, clearing away the debris, and
making room for the fire of the soul, but it would not last. As his attention returned to the words
on the page, another voice coiled and wrapped around his throat, like a vine twisting and strangling
until the fire was suffocated. It spoke just five words, and the mad philosopher found his hands
typing without his consent.

"Obituary of the Free Will"

He began to scream in pain, and picked the typewriter up, before slamming it into the desk over and
over. The orderlies rushed forward to stop him, but even as they pulled him to the ground, he
continued to thrash and beat the typewriter against the floor, doing whatever he could to destroy
the machine that held his mind.

"Barney, the page!" snapped the director through the intercom.

As the other orderly administered a sedative and the mad philosopher went limp, Barney picked up the
mangled typewriter and pulled the white sheet from the wreck. It was intact, if a little crumpled,
and every word could be read.

"We have it" he said.

In his office the director released a sigh, and took a moment to pour a drink from his crystal
decanter. He returned to his desk and spoke again.

"Acknowledged. Bring it to me, and see Mr. Pendil back to his cell."

Later that day the director read the page over from start to end over and over, trying his best to
make sense of his patient's condition. His notes read:

"Experimentation on Patient 4443 continues to yield a positive response to psycho-chrora-hexane. In
all other subjects administration of even a subclinical dose causes permanent nerve damage and
paralysis; however, patient 4443 appears to be metabolizing the drug and entering a state of altered
consciousness. I have sent tissue samples to Dr. Brenner at Statesville University for analysis. If
we can identify what makes his biology unique, we might be able to stabilize the reaction and move
on to a real stage 3 trial. Next test will commence next week once subject has had time to recover."

He put away his pen, walked over to the bookshelf, and retrieved a binder labeled "Subject 4443:
Arthur Pendil". He filed away his notes, stepped onto the balcony and surveyed the grounds of the
Statesville Institute, watching as the patients slowly drifted around the lush garden and took in
the bright summer's day. He recognized each, and he knew their conditions well, for he was nothing
if not meticulous. Eventually they would all be healed, he thought, as his mind worked over Arthur's
letter and his unique ability to channel madness into thought. He watched as the patients drifted,
aimlessly, and hopelessly about, and reminded himself: For the greater good go I. As his mind
churned with the ethical implications of his work, another was awakening deep within the building.
The mad philosopher's eyes bolted open and he sat upright in his padded cell. He felt the familiar
embrace of his straitjacket, but gone were the shackles that held his mind back. Where once was fog
now lived a singular, focused drive:

"I will be free," he said aloud with vicious fury. "I, WILL be free."
