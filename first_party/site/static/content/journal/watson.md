Watson sat behind his desk and closed his eyes. It was a grand desk, befitting only a grand person,
and he was certainly a worthy candidate. At least, so he thought, and his guru regularly reminded
him to channel only good energy. As he leaned back in his chair (Herman Miller, of course), he
bathed in the sunlight from the enormous window overlooking the company campus, and let his thoughts
clear, until only a positive self-image remained. He interlinked his fingers and smiled to himself,
for it was the third Tuesday of the quarter, and that meant it was his favorite day: Performance
review, or as he thought of it, privately, people maximization. It was time to cull the weak and
reward the strong, so as the sunlight warmed his face and bled into his smile, he got to work.

He opened his laptop. It showed the performance ratings of all his staff, stack ranked from best
(himself of course) to worst (a poor fellow named Rajeevn). He briefly wondered: How could an 'n'
follow a 'v', before reminding himself how antidiverse he was being in that moment. He looked to his
left, to a reminder of what truly mattered. On the wall hung a motto:

"Diverse or Die"

At FizzBuzz Industries, diversity was not simply a value, it was a virtue, and it must be signaled
at every turn. It must be mentioned in every keynote, broadcast in every memo, and championed in
every meeting. If ever the company should find itself being antidiverse, it would surely indicate a
deep failure, and no one should rest until the source had been found, put on a PIP, and managed out.
It's just good business, and in the end good business means good profits. If there was just anything
worth taking from the 80s, apart from all the cocaine, it's the belief that greed is good. God how
he missed the 80s.

Watson snapped out of his trip down memory lane and got back to work. Grateful for the reminder to
be inclusive of other cultures and their unconventional spellings, he resumed his work cleaning
house. A name such as Rajeevn was certainly acceptable, cherished even, for why shouldn't a 'v'
precede an 'n'? He scheduled a whipping appointment on his calendar to appropriately chastise
himself, then, satisfied that he was being appropriately inclusive, proceeded to press the delete
button on Rajeevn. Three floors away security were notified, and scrambled to work immediately, lest
their own performance be marked down. Moments later, a short Indian man from the UX committee was
stripped of his company-issued clothes in the middle of the office, escorted from the premises, and
unceremoniously thrown to the curb with nothing but the tie around his neck to protect his modesty.

A loading spinner disappeared from Watson's computer to be replaced with a green tick.

"Performance reviewed!" the computer chirped, and Watson smiled with glee.

It felt good to be inclusive, and he was confident his newly reduced team included only the best and
brightest. He popped open a Coca-Cola and strolled through the office, ready to get down to
business, whatever it was the company was actually doing right now. He approached a manager,
selected purely at random (definitely not because of her very large breasts), and asked what her
team was doing. The manager stood to attention, like the good little soldier she was, and directed
Watson to the nearby conference room. The plaque next to the door read "Lovelace", a tribute to one
of tech's greatest female champions. He stepped inside to collaborate, wondering what kind of
innovations the company would produce today.

The reaction from the staff: None, not even a turned head. "Good", he thought, hierarchy was
obsolete, and he should be treated no differently from an intern. He made a note to inform building
operations though, for the team was sweating profusely, and a broken AC system was the last problem
they needed to worry about. He put aside thoughts of trivial maintenance and turned his attention to
the future, to the team, and the spirit of innovation.

The employees continued their discussion and Watson listened. He made mental notes as they discussed
project status, came to consensus on technical topics, and argued about challenges involving UI
padding and font alignment. Such sacred ideals, he thought, the user must come first. FIRST! Not
second, not third, not fourth, not fif- He was snapped out of his internal monologue by a question
from a new hire. He squinted, doing his best to remember the employee's name, Jared wasn't it? Yes
Jared, with the red hair, Ja-RED, brilliant system, he thought. The question though. Mmm, no. There
was much room to improve here.

"Why are we doing this?" asked Jared.

Another began to speak but Watson sensed an opportunity to lead. He stood and used his best manager
voice, strong, commanding, but without authority, never with authority. Authority was for the likes
of IBM, Intel, and all the other has-beens.

"An organization is born. Like a small child it stumbles. It falls. It learns to walk. In time it
finds its way, learns to run, sprint, and eventually leap and bound. It builds momentum, flourishes,
and achieves greatness. Its members flourish and its dependents are satisfied, both finding
enjoyment as they serve the greater whole and are served by it. It's a beautiful sight to behold,
but it doesn't last forever, for inevitably mistakes accrue, and cracks form."

The team was quiet, listening, and making notes frantically trying to capture the wisdom they were
about to receive (or ensure it looked that way). Watson pressed on.

"Eventually the organization is trapped in a maze of its own making. Vines which once grew freely
bend and twist through the corridors of process and bureaucracy. Endless hallways of doors form, but
the keys are lost and the people are divided. All yearning for free growth, all wishing for a return
to the simpler days of genuine humanity, but none aware of their own contribution to the maze."

A senior-staff software engineer nodded with a gleam in his eye. He had waited for many cycles to
hear a speech from Watson in person, and had almost peed himself in joy at this opportunity to drink
the wisdom straight from the source. Watson gestured to the people in the room with outstretched
arms.

"Humanity is the source of all innovation, and, sadly, its ultimate self-destruction. We seek
prosperity but act on greed, destroying the good will and genuine excitement that spawned the
initial growth. We seek novelty but act on impulse, destroying the curiosity that set the
imagination ablaze in the early days. We seek stability but find only stagnation, destroying the
will to be challenged and grow. Humanity creates innovation, and humanity destroys it!"

A woman, a UX engineer named Jenny, raised her hand to ask a question, but was quickly saved by her
colleague, another man named Remi, who instinctively pushed it down before Watson could notice.
Watson continued his monologue, his eyes glazing over and staring through the rainbow paint on the
walls.

"What does the lone innovator do in a maze of bureaucracy? They can see the pattern, but how do they
respond? They can find the exit, but how do they open the door? They can unite with others, but how
do they move ahead as one? Questions which must be answered to innovate again, and questions which
hold the answer. For the question is the ultimate source of innovation."

With that his work was done. He spun on his heel, turned to the exit, and continued on his way. The
path of the tech executive was laden with challenges, but he was a great man, a great man with a
great bank account to prove it, and no challenge was beyond him. As he roamed the halls, searching
for a problem to solve or a team to optimize, it never occurred to him that every labyrinth needs a
minotaur.
