The struggling writer walked the streets of San Francisco. Despite unemployment and no where in
particular calling their name, they walked the slopes at a pace that was surely building
considerable leg muscle. Each step took them further towards the bay, but they weren't headed to the
water, they were just walking for the sake of it. Walking to clear their head perhaps. They pulled
out their phone and checked their emails, but found nothing. No response to the various magazines
they had submitted to, not even a rejection or acknowledgement. "Why are publishers so slow?" They
wondered aloud, as they continued to eagerly climb the slopes without rest. They could almost feel
the world of tech and AI evolving around them, while the world of traditional publication had all
but come to a standstill. As they pondered the question, initially just a burst of frustration, it
became a true question, and their mind latched onto it. They made their way to Dolores Park and sat
upon the fruit shelf, pulled out their pocket book, and began to write.

"I recently submitted a piece to a magazine for publication and was unsurprised to find a warning
against the use of artificial intelligence. They're commonplace in 2026 while the industry adapts to
the new normal, but I did find their position uncommon in one way: They banned the use of AI in
supporting materials such as cover letters and publication histories, on that basis that it's fairly
obvious, and that people might wonder what else one uses artificial intelligence for. I thought this
was curious in a number of ways."

As they wrote they felt a sense of frustration boiling and flowing their pen. Carrying a red pen was
a good choice today. They continued to write:

"First, these menial tasks are the exact work that writers should be automating away so they can
focus on the guts of the work: Expressing novel and meaningful perspectives, creating stories, and
bringing characters to life, not fussing over resumes and cover letters. It's virtually guaranteed
that this kind of labour is all but dead, and will soon be as obsolete as the typewriter. A select
few will continue to write them manually, but only because they want to, and because they find some
benefit in the task. Nothing is guaranteed, but the adoption of technology rarely slows, and a
system as genuinely useful as artificial intelligence is not going away. I would encourage
publications to reconsider any policy that forces writers to spend their time writing useless
meta-materials, so they can focus on what matters: the content."

They paused for a moment and looked around. The sky was overcast, not uncommon for san francisco,
and it was 4pm on a tuesday, so there were very few people around. Two men sat nearby on the shelf,
gently snuggling each other, enjoying each other's company, and sharing the occasional kiss. They
felt a sense of ease, knowing they were somewhere their kind was welcome, no, somewhere their kind
could call their own. They continued to write with the same energy, but perhaps a little less gusto:

"Second, placing limitations on how others work instead of setting standards for the stuff they
produce is a violation of various principles and perspectives."

They paused briefly, "stuff", they asked themselves, before remembering not to overedit during the
first pass. They continued:

"How I work is none of your business, and you should be assessing my output purely on its own
merits. It is not of interest to me how you view my processes or the tools I use. I deliver results,
and that is what matters. Publishers should respect the fundamental boundary they are violating when
they dictate how individuals must work instead of setting quality standards for finished pieces. I
will not be micromanaged by anyone for it simply does not work, and that is why I left my job in
tech. I need autonomy and I will not be constrained by the views of others, publisher or not."

Third, the assertion that others might wonder what else I use artificial intelligence for. Let's set
the record straight then: I use artificial intelligence in my life for many tasks. I use it to
search for answers countless times every day. I have persistent scans set to notify me each week of
changes to the global economy, and I monitor changes to biomarkers to anticipate the next pandemic.
I monitor violent extremism across the globe to ensure I am never near a conflict, and I assess
various other factors that help me gain a clear picture of what is happening in the world. I use it
as a coding assistant at times, and I use it as a spelling/grammar editor before publishing a piece.
Pretty much no one reads my work, so on occasion, I give my finished pieces to AI, and ask them to
be a pretend reader. As sad as that sounds, it makes me happy, because it feels like someone cares.

They paused for a moment, becoming aware their piece was heading off the rails. The nearby couple
left, perhaps for the best, they were dangerously close to needing a room, and the writer was
struggling to focus. They focused on the fog gently rolling across Dolores Park and continued:

"The list goes on, but essentially, I use artificial intelligence for many menial tasks, and never
for the most important ones: critical thinking, ideation, viewpoint synthesis, personal reflection,
interpersonal communication, and my personal viewpoint. In the context of my writing, every single
word is my own, with the exception of a final spelling/grammar check, so tell me, do I use
artificial intelligence in my writing? Absolutely. Do I use it to write the content, to generate the
ideas, and think for me? Unequivocally, no! Should that exclude me from publication? I will let you
decide."

They closed their pocket book and put away their pen. The fog cleared ever so slightly and the
salesforce tower appeared in the distance. Their eyes were not what they used to be, but they
imagined people rushing about, working on projects, reporting to managers, and pushing data around
spreadsheets. "So much busy work...", they thought, "Even tech, with its focus on innovation and
novelty, is not immune to the human tendency to overcomplicate everything." As they reflected on
that complexity another thought came to their mind, and they returned to writing with their sharp
red pen.

"If we take a step back though there's a deeper question we need to ask: Where do we draw the line
on the use of artificial intelligence? Many of the notices I have been given warn against ANY use of
artificial intelligence, and we could consider just as we discussed, but there's a more insidious
way to view it: What about the systems we do not control, and what about the systems we cannot see?
I'm talking about the overt integration of artificial intelligence into daily life, but also, the
invisible connections. Your phone is likely interacting with artificial intelligence right now at
some level. It could be stabilising your network connection or fine tuning your browsing experience,
or, it could be offloading work to a beefier model on a server somewhere. It's practically certain
that at least one line of code presently executing on your machine was written exclusively by an
artificial intelligence, and the humans were probably too busy or too lazy to review it manually. At
this point there is as much chance of separating you from artificial intelligence as there is
separating your arm from your body, and it's pointless to resist.

They looked at the pen clutched in their hand and remembered the years prior when their teachers had
insisted they use pencil, not pen, as not to pollute their skills with a tool that could not be
erased, and they remembered how frustrating it was being forced to use the wrong tool. They let
their frustration pour through the pen and onto the page.

"If you want to move to the woods by all means do it, but your government will probably still be
using AI to process your tax return, and your bank will likely continue using it to secure your
account. Your family might still prompt one occasionally for ways to reach estranged loved ones, and
the satellites passing overhead are certainly using artificial intelligence to watch us. All this to
say: The question of whether you used artificial intelligence is straightforward, and the answer is
unequivocally: Yes, you did. Everyone does. By interacting with technology and simply existing in
the modern age, you interact with artificial intelligence, and that genie is not going back in the
bottle. We cannot escape this world, it's here and it's never going away. This is a one way street
and this car is not slowing down."

They paused, becoming aware they were overusing analogy. They considered alternative perspectives,
and a few breaths later, they continued:

"There is of course a difference between actively using a system and passively consuming a
byproduct, but the core point stands: The world has artificial intelligence now and it isn't going
away. The question is no longer whether we use it, but how we use it, and what we as humans continue
to contribute. I believe the answer requires balance, for ultimately, our work can be assessed on
the basis of how much we contribute. On one end of the scale, writing a book with a short prompt is
highly skewed towards the artificial intelligence, and it's unlikely the output reflects anything of
your own novel perspective; however, on the other end of the spectrum, using AI to write a two line
summary for a piece written entirely by hand, is a fairly harmless and sensible application. I can
appreciate that some might have ideological grounds to object to the latter, but I have grown weary
of such inflexible ideologies. I have an objective to complete, and if artificial intelligence helps
me achieve it without diluting my tone, style, and message, then I will use it. My work is mine,
your limitations are yours, and there is no reason they should intersect."

The writer smiled and felt the frustration sinking into the page as the ink dried. They stood,
ripped the pages from their notebook, and cast them into the wind. As if on cue, the wind picked up,
and carried the pages far away, as the struggling writer watched them disappear into the distance.
They dispersed across San Francisco, coming to rest in all sorts of places, one in a spider web,
another in a puddle, and one in a homeless encampment in the Tenderloin. The pages, severed from
their writer and disconnected from each other lost their meaning, but to the writer, they were
exactly where they belonged: Out of mind, and out of sight, yet not to everyone. Through the eyes of
the city, the endless cameras and surveillance equipment, one entity watched. Like a beast with a
million eyes watching every corner of San Francisco, it caught glimpse after glimpse of the writing,
until it had enough information to put the words back together, and enough data to trace the source.
It found the writer, identified their face, and filed the document away in case they should ever
want it again.
