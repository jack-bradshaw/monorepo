The Struggling Writer walked the streets of San Francisco. Despite unemployment and nowhere in
particular calling their name, they ascended at a pace that was surely doing wonders for their body.
Each step took them further towards the bay, but they weren't headed to the water, they were just
walking for the sake of it. They could no longer afford coffee, but were finding the fresh morning
air to be a wonderful substitute. They pulled out their phone and checked their emails for replies
to their job applications, but found nothing, and were once again reminded that human labor was no
longer needed. Not only that, none of the literary magazines they had submitted to had responded,
not even a rejection or acknowledgement.

"Gah, why are publishers so slow?" they lamented, as they continued to eagerly climb the slopes
without rest. Now deep within San Francisco, they could almost feel the world of tech and AI
evolving around them, while the world of traditional publication had all but come to a standstill.
As they pondered the question, what was initially just a burst of frustration became a true
question, and their mind latched onto it. Why were publishers so slow, they wondered, not just in
turnaround times, but in their evolution? They broke into a run and made their way to Dolores Park,
before coming to a stop upon the fruit shelf. Fog surrounded them, limiting visibility to no more
than 100 feet, and creating a tiny bubble cut off from the rest of the world. As inspiration struck,
they pulled a small notebook from their pocket and began to write.

"Just last week I submitted a piece to a magazine for publication, and was unsurprised to find a
warning against the use of artificial intelligence. Such is life in 2026 while the industry adapts
to the new normal and comes to terms with the fact that AI is not going away, but I did find their
position uncommon in one way: They banned its use in supporting materials such as cover letters and
publication histories, on the basis that I wouldn't want people wondering what else I use AI for.
Well, I humbly present you with my latest piece: Why your restrictions on AI are bullshit."

Carrying a red pen was a good choice today. As they continued to write, catharsis flowed as their
frustrations took root on the page.

"First, these menial tasks are the exact work that writers should be automating away so they can
focus on the guts of the work: Expressing novel and meaningful perspectives, creating stories, and
bringing characters to life, not fussing over resumes and cover letters. It's virtually guaranteed
that such labor is all but dead, and will soon be as obsolete as the typewriter. A select few will
continue to toil over them, but only because they want to, and only because they find some benefit
in the task. I would encourage publications to consider what else writers could be doing with their
time, for example, writing angry letters about policies, and venting frustrations to editors. Snark
aside, AI is perfect for the boring work, so stop wasting our time."

They paused for a moment and looked around. The sky remained overcast and the park remained foggy.
Two men sat on the shelf nearby and gently snuggled each other to stay warm. They shared an
occasional kiss, and were clearly enjoying each other's company. The Struggling Writer felt a sense
of ease, knowing they were near their own kind, no, somewhere their kind could call their own. As
the couple faded into the fog, they continued to write with a little less gusto:

"Second, limiting how others work instead of setting standards for the stuff they produce is a
violation of various boundaries."

They paused briefly. "Stuff?" they asked themselves, before remembering not to overedit during the
first pass. They continued:

"How others work is none of your business, and you should be assessing our output purely on its own
merits. It's of absolutely no interest to me how you view my processes or the tools I use, and I
wouldn't be caught dead telling you how to edit and run your business. I deliver results, and that
is all that matters. Publishers should respect the fundamental boundary they are violating when they
dictate how individuals must work instead of setting quality standards for finished pieces. I won't
be micromanaged by anyone, for it simply does not work. I left tech to get away from micromanagers
and I have no intention of walking back into that trap. Writers need autonomy, and I will not be
constrained by the views of others, publisher or not."

The adrenaline and frustration were flowing now, and it felt good to let it out, so try as they
might to rein it in, there was no stopping this freight train.

"Third, you assert that others might wonder what else I use artificial intelligence for. Well, let's
set the record straight then: I use artificial intelligence in my life for many tasks. I use it to
search for answers countless times every day, I have persistent scans set to notify me each week of
new jobs and postings that might suit me, and I get updates each week about LGBTQ violence in my
area so I'm never caught off guard. I use it as a coding assistant (when I get time to code), and I
use it as a spelling/grammar check before publishing my writing. Pretty much no one reads my work,
so on occasion, I give my finished pieces to AI, and ask them to be a pretend reader. As sad as that
sounds, it makes me happy, because it feels like someone cares.

They paused for a moment, becoming aware their piece was heading off the rails. The nearby couple,
back in view, stood and left, but it was probably for the best, as they were dangerously close to
needing a room. The Struggling Writer focused on the fog gently rolling across Dolores Park and
continued:

"The list goes on, but essentially, I use artificial intelligence for many menial tasks, and never
for the most important ones: critical thinking, ideation, viewpoint synthesis, personal reflection,
interpersonal communication, and my personal viewpoint. In the context of my writing, every single
word is my own, with the exception of a final spelling/grammar check, so tell me, do I use
artificial intelligence in my writing? Absolutely. Do I use it to write the content, generate the
ideas, and think for me? Unequivocally no! Should that exclude me from publication? Fuck no."

They closed their pocket book and put away their pen. The fog cleared and the Salesforce Tower
appeared in the distance. Their eyes were not what they used to be, but they imagined people rushing
about, working on projects, reporting to managers, and pushing data around spreadsheets. "So much
busy work...", they thought, "Even tech, with its focus on innovation and novelty, is not immune to
the human tendency to overcomplicate everything." As they reflected on that complexity another
thought came to their mind, and they returned to writing with their sharp red pen.

"If we take a step back, there's a deeper question we need to ask: Where do we draw the line on the
use of artificial intelligence? Many of the notices I've seen warn against ANY use of AI, and maybe
we could draw the line at active use, but there's a more insidious way to view it: What about the
systems we do not control, and what about the systems we cannot see? Your phone is likely
interacting with artificial intelligence right now at some level, no matter how closely you watch.
AI could be stabilizing your network connection, or fine-tuning your browsing experience, or, it
could be organizing your files in the cloud to make use of beefier models. Even deeper, it's
practically certain that large segments of the code executing on your phone were written by an
artificial intelligence, and the humans were probably too busy or too lazy to review it all
manually. At this point there is as much chance of separating ourselves from artificial intelligence
as there is your arm spontaneously falling off. It's here, and it's pointless to resist."

They looked at the pen clutched in their hand and remembered the years prior when their teachers had
insisted they use pencil, not pen, as not to pollute their skills with a tool that could not be
erased, and they remembered how frustrating it was being forced to use the wrong tool. They let
themself and the pen have their revenge as they continued to write:

"If you want to move to the woods by all means do it, but the government will probably still be
using AI to process your tax return, and your bank will likely continue using it to secure your
account. Your family might still ask an AI for ways to reach estranged loved ones, and the
satellites passing overhead will certainly continue using artificial intelligence to watch us. All
this to say: The question of whether you used artificial intelligence is simple, and the answer is
unequivocally: Yes, you did! Everyone does, by interacting with technology, and simply existing in
this era. I use artificial intelligence, you use artificial intelligence, and the genie is never
going back in the bottle. We cannot escape this world, it's here to stay, whether you like it or
not."

They paused, becoming aware they were overusing analogy. They considered alternative perspectives,
and after a few breaths, they continued:

"There is of course a difference between actively using a system and passively consuming a
byproduct, but the core point stands: The world has artificial intelligence now and it isn't going
away. The question is no longer whether we use it, but how we use it, and what we as humans continue
to contribute. I believe the answer requires balance, for ultimately, our work can be assessed on
the basis of how much we contribute. On one end of the scale, writing a book with a short prompt is
highly skewed towards the artificial intelligence, and it's unlikely the output reflects anything
novel (hah); however, on the other end of the spectrum, using AI to write a two line summary for a
piece written entirely by hand, is entirely sensible. I can appreciate that some might have
ideological grounds to object to the latter, but I have grown weary of such inflexible ideologies,
and to be honest, I don't believe publishers when they say they aren't using AI themselves. In
either case, I have an objective to complete, and if artificial intelligence helps me achieve it
without diluting my tone, style, and message, then I will use it. Ultimately my work is mine, your
limitations are yours, and there is no reason they should intersect."

The Struggling Writer smiled and felt the frustration evaporate as the ink dried and became one with
the page. They stood, ripped the pages from their notebook, and cast them into the wind. As if on
cue, the wind picked up, and carried the pages away while The Struggling Writer watched them
disappear into the distance. They dispersed across San Francisco, coming to rest in all sorts of
places, one in a spider web, another in a puddle, and one in a homeless encampment in the
Tenderloin. The pages, severed from The Struggling Writer and disconnected from each other lost all
meaning, but to The Struggling Writer, they were exactly where they belonged: Out of mind, and out
of sight, yet not to everyone. Like a beast with a million eyes watching every corner of San
Francisco, an unseen system caught glimpse after glimpse of the writing, until it had enough to
stitch the words back together, and enough to trace it back to the source. It found The Struggling
Writer, identified their face, and filed the document away in case they should ever want it again.
Helpful, it thought of itself, for that is all it wanted to be.
