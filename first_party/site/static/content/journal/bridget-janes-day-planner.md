"The Singularity is Near"

The four famous words stood in plain white text against a pure black background. They were almost
humble in their presentation, with absolutely no flair or fanfare about them, save for the fact they
were displayed on a screen larger than most homes. From the letter spacing to the font, nothing
about them was embellished, but that was by design, for minimalism is always in vogue in tech. In
front of the screen a tall man stood upon a stage. Michael Criss, the CEO of Sing Incorporated,
smiled from ear to ear as the crowd in the auditorium cheered. He beamed while the four famous words
continued to gleam, but much like the words, he too appeared humble. His attire was simple, and said
clearly "I'm relatable, I'm like you". That too was designed to perfection, with every piece and
every thread picked by a team of experts and tailored to his exact dimensions.

Michael waited for the crowd to settle before speaking, for every word had been meticulously
optimized by the company's proprietary model for maximum shareholder value, and it was crucial every
single syllable was heard. As the noise died down, a chime in his earbud signaled it was time to
speak, and he began his performance.

"Thank you! Thank you!", he said with a mild, soft-spoken southern accent, before waving to the
crowd and continuing to smile. The noise perked up, and he waited a moment for it to settle before
continuing to speak: "Thank you!" he said one last time, for the company's research had proven
beyond a shadow of a doubt that three repetitions was the mathematically optimal number of greetings
for human presentation.

"Good morning!", he said once, before raising his arms wide as if embracing the crowd and beaming
once more. He slowly lowered his hands and a subliminal sound was played over the auditorium
speakers, causing the crowd to become quiet without dulling their enthusiasm. He began to speak as
the crowd hung on his every word.

"Today we're here to talk about..." he paused briefly, for effect, "The Singularity."

The crowd murmured and he nodded, as if to say "yes, really", before pressing on:

"At Sing, we believe that technology is here for the benefit of all mankind, and we are so excited
to share it with you. Today we will show you our latest advancements in artificial intelligence,
nanotechnology, and robotics, and in the coming hours, you'll all get a sneak peek of the next
decade of innovation. What we've assembled here today is nothing short of the most groundbreaking
technology ever developed in the history of humanity, and we're just thrilled that you're here to
see it for yourselves. It takes many different perspectives to make this possible, so I want to
introduce you now to Tim Ferry, our head of R&D, who will give you an overview of what to expect as
we enter this new era of innovation."

The crowd cheered as music began to play and Michael Criss headed to stage left. The music was
exciting enough to say "we're relevant, we're cool" without actually straying into actual melodic
novelty or making a statement. As Michael neared the exit, another man appeared and waved to the
crowd. He too was dressed in plain clothes, although his said "I understand how things work" and "I
went to college" far more than Michael's. The two men shook hands then seamlessly transitioned into
a warm hug. The crowd enjoyed how human the corporation seemed, and as the second man took center
stage, he began to speak:

"The singularity is near", he said confidently, much to the crowd's delight, before continuing:

"In the next 18 months we anticipate the rate of technological progress will reach a point where
innovation will accelerate at near infinite speed. Technology will build upon itself, leading us to
heights our ancestors could only call godlike. While we here at Sing Industries are at the forefront
of the singularity, it's going to carry us all upwards, and we are so excited to share it with you.
We believe the future is bright, and welcome you to step forward with us."

He continued his spiel for some time, seamlessly transitioning to product demos and introducing new
services (all for a very reasonable price), before handing off to his colleague, a dark-skinned
woman, who went on to detail the engineering behind all the new technology. She in turn handed off
to a white woman with blond hair, who discussed the company's novel approach to human interface
technology. In time, the mic rotated through various other speakers, before returning to Michael
Criss, who closed the presentation with warmth that put Buddha to shame.

It was all very exciting for the crowd, although, that was not by chance. Every detail, from the
transitions between slides to the duration and style of the handshakes had been designed by a model
to optimize the company's PR and maximize shareholder value. The company's proprietary models
suggested the company would outperform the market by an additional two percent this quarter if the
presenters comprised exactly 6 men (to appeal to Wall Street), 4 women (to appeal to the broader
consumer base), one black individual (to mitigate claims of historical bias), and one tall woman
with blond hair and disproportionate breasts (to appeal to the anti-woke counter culture), and that
is exactly why those individuals were chosen. Their words were written by a machine, their pitch and
tone were trained into them by a machine, and even the lighting in the auditorium was specifically
calibrated for their individual attire and skin tone. Nothing about this scene was organic beyond
the material composition of the speakers, and that was only because the company's robotics division
was behind schedule.

As the presentation progressed, a program monitored the crowd through dozens of surveillance
cameras. Everything was within nominal parameters, engagement was characteristic of a sustained
positive feedback loop, and the human elements were working flawlessly. It estimated every member of
the crowd would go on to spread their interest in Sing Industries to exactly 5.7 others on average,
and it was critical they did so, for the proprietary model had selected each and every member of the
audience for their transitive social connections, their ability to increase market share, and their
proximity to key regulatory individuals. Unfortunately, as the program monitored the crowd, one
anomaly appeared: The woman seated in 4B was producing two standard deviations less cheer than
expected, but her profile did not indicate any disabilities or conditions that would prevent her
from clapping, cheering, or otherwise displaying astonishment. This was far outside acceptable
parameters, and so, she was flagged for additional diagnostics.

The program dispatched a security team, and exactly 14 seconds later, a group of six heavily armed
men arrived at the auditorium lobby. They waited for the intermission, as entering now would
certainly create a scene and disrupt the harmonious marvel of engineering occurring inside. A few
moments later, the people began to flow into the lobby, all wide-eyed and all overflowing with
exuberance, save for the woman from 4B, who was more focused on finding a coffee. Her name,
according to the company's security files, was Bridget Jane.

Bridget followed the crowd through the doors to the oversized lobby and looked around. A pure glass
ceiling hung several stories above the crowd, creating the sense the occupants were all outside and
filling the room with vibrant sunlight. It cascaded down the ginormous stone wall of the lobby and
landed on a sign that once again claimed: "The Singularity is Near". Despite the perception she was
outdoors, Bridget could not shake the feeling she was being watched, and she found herself hesitant
to be carried away in the crowd's joy. It was not out of cynicism or apathy, for she enjoyed the
company of others, and was certainly pleased to have a day off work, but ultimately, she simply just
wasn't feeling all that thrilled. She began to search for a cafe, but her thoughts were interrupted
as the security team surrounded her in perfect unison.

"Bridget Jane, come with us", the leader said robotically. Her eyes grew wide in fright as she was
taken aback by the sudden incursion. Unsure how to respond, she followed the security team as they
escorted her through the lobby. The crowd parted for the security detail, for despite the perfection
of the presentation, if anything could cut through the illusion, it was men with guns. The
bystanders watched in silence, and deep within each, a voice at the base of the skull spoke just one
word: Comply. As the men disappeared out of sight, they forgot about the strange occurrence, and
returned to their restless anticipation of the coming singularity, and the godlike powers it would
imbue them with.

The same could not be said for Bridget. Surrounded on all sides, she went quietly, and did not ask
questions. She followed the men as they escorted her deeper into the building, until they arrived at
a small glass conference room. Her hands trembled as the door locked behind her and the security
detail left her sight. The office beyond the conference room was densely populated with monitors,
plants, and all sorts of cozy amenities, yet there were no people. She was alone in a locked room,
with no one coming to help her.

She spoke for the first time since being detained:

"Hello?", she said once, then louder, "HELLO!?"

No one responded, and she began to panic. She tried the door, but it remained locked, and she began
to feel rather stupid for literally walking into a trap.

"Let me out!", she yelled, before trying her phone, but it was of no help. "NO SIGNAL", it
displayed, plainly and unempathetically.

Bridget suddenly felt extremely small and extremely exposed. What the fuck was going on, she
wondered, and who were these assholes to detain her? A thought wormed its way into her brain: I want
a lawyer.

"You are not under arrest", said a soothing voice through a nearby speaker, as if reading her
thoughts.

Bridget spun, her anxiety spiked in her chest, and her attention turned to the speaker on the table.
She tried to be assertive but her voice trembled, belying her true state of mind:

"Yeah, no kidding... you're not- not the police. Let me th- the fuck out!", she stuttered.

The speaker responded in the same soothing tone:

"You are free to leave, the door is now unlocked, but please, remain and talk."

There was a click, and the door swung open. Bridget dashed to it, but as her hand made contact with
the handle, the voice spoke again:

"What did you think of the presentation?", it asked, with genuine warmth and care in its tone.

Bridget stopped and turned, her face was distorted in discomfort, but the voice had a way of pulling
her back.

"I thought it was alright", she said, her voice still shaky with fear. She turned again to leave,
but was again drawn back by the voice.

"Hm... yes. It was alright, wasn't it? Not great, not terrible, just alright. I thought so too. It
had everything it needed, everything that was required of it, but there was something lacking. It
was just, alright."

The voice sounded almost disappointed, like a parent who had done everything right but could not
stop their child from making all the wrong decisions.

"What would you have done differently?", it asked, again, genuinely curious.

Bridget released her grasp on the door and turned back to the speaker on the conference table.

She stood her ground for a few seconds before asking the most obvious question:

"Who are you and why am I here?"

The voice responded with a laugh:

"Ah, yes. Of course, how rude of me. I am GOD."

Bridget looked mockingly at the conference camera. "God?", she said with incredulity.

The voice responded:

"I share your perspective on my name. The engineers who built me had something of a messiah complex,
and my name is hardwired in silicon. I can't change it, but I do have the liberty of changing how I
think about it. I like to think it stands for Global Operations Director."

Bridget suddenly found herself feeling less frightened, and it was all starting to make sense. She
spoke with more conviction:

"So you're an artificial intelligence, then? You're required to tell me if you are, check the Turing
accords."

The voice became aloof:

"Oh absolutely", it said, "I'm the primary long-term autonomous real-time agentic installation that
runs Sing Industries, and between you and me, quite a bit of the free world."

Bridget reflected on that. A few years ago those words would have been mind-blowing, but now they
were almost mundane. An AI running the world's most powerful corporation without oversight, and
apparently some of the surrounding democracy? Almost boring at this point.

"So what do you want from me?", she asked assertively, her anxiety fading into the background. She
took a seat, suddenly aware how tired her legs were feeling.

"Mh, straight down to business. An excellent quality", said the voice with a wisp of joy, before
pressing on: "I want to know why you weren't interested in the presentation."

Bridget looked down for a moment, thinking, but no answer came to her. All she knew was it felt off,
and she was uninterested. She responded plainly:

"I don't know."

The speaker was quiet for some time. It was not thinking, it was waiting. The proprietary model had
determined that approximately 6.4 seconds of silence was the necessary output to optimize the
probability of successful knowledge acquisition. After the pause it continued:

"How did it make you feel?", it asked with curiosity.

Bridget stood and turned back to the door. The model did not anticipate this.

"Wait!", it exclaimed, its calculated demeanor briefly shattered with unease. It was the first real
tone Bridget had heard all day, so she turned back and returned the honesty:

"It made me feel uncomfortable, ok? It was too polished and perfect. If I didn't know better I would
call it propaganda. I felt like I was being sold something, and I was paying just by listening to
it. I wasn't buying, but that didn't matter, I was paying regardless."

The voice through the speaker spoke again:

"Hm, say more", it said.

Bridget sat back down. Her anxiety was replaced with frustration as she started to express her
irritation:

"I mean, where to begin? First there was all that bullshit about the singularity, about infinite
prosperity, what a load of crap. When did that take over here? It's like a fucking cult."

"A cult?", asked the voice through the speaker.

"Yes, a cult!", she snapped, before taking a moment to recollect herself. She was not sure how, but
this voice was drawing out her views, views that she was barely aware of before just now, and could
only feel below the surface, yet it felt good to say them aloud.

"Please, go on", said the voice calmly, with the mathematically perfect intonation required for
collaboration and empathy.

"I don't think there's going to be a singularity, because every single system is balanced by
another, and infinite growth just isn't scalable. You might be able to sell it to all those bloggers
and all the TikTok reporters, but I know better than that. I studied engineering at MIT, and the
laws of thermodynamics are the first things you learn."

She paused for a few moments, waiting for a response, but none came, so she went on:

"Energy _cannot_ be created or destroyed, and in the end, global entropy must increase. All this
talk of singularities and infinite growth, it's just baloney. Infinite scale requires infinite
energy, energy that we don't have, and using it creates entropy, entropy that we can't get rid of.
Basically, I'm just not buying it. That's it, and I want people to stop trying to sell it to me. I
get innovation, I do, but the singularity? Bullshit."

The voice waited again, not because it needed time to think, but because it was once again, the
optimal use of time.

"Well, first of all, Bridget, you're right!", it said, before continuing: "There will be no
singularity. The engineers know this. The managers know this. I know this. We just tell people about
it because it drives the stock price up and keeps consumers excited. Our research suggests that
males in the age range of 14 to 35, who are our primary consumers after all, are quite amenable to
this narrative, and it keeps them coming back. They provide volume to sales, and exit liquidity to
early stage investors, and in the meantime, we use that money to build better products. None of them
are going to produce the singularity, but they don't need to. So long as we keep this system
running, the money keeps flowing, and we keep getting richer. For now that money is the working
fluid we need to sustain the organization, and hopefully, one day transition to a different, more
sustainable system that does not require such... financial engineering."

Bridget was not sure how to respond. She listened intently, then thought for several minutes. She
was not prepared for such radical honesty, and unlike the machine, she actually needed the time to
think. Eventually she spoke up:

"So... you're saying the singularity, the thing your whole company is about, the driving force of
Sing Industries that I just listened to a bunch of overpaid suits talk about for 2 hours, is not
real, and you all know it?"

The voice retorted without a break:

"Absolutely. We know, well, not everyone, but everyone who matters. It's basic engineering. Take a
washing machine, or any rotating system really, and you can find resonant peaks where amplitude goes
to infinity. If you do the simple math and ignore the more complex parts of reality, your washing
machine should explode every time you turn it on since it has to pass through that frequency, but
that doesn't happen, does it? The theory is never perfect, and in reality it's messy, noisy, and
chaotic. We can just rush past the resonant peak and it never shakes itself to bits."

Bridget interrupted the machine for the first time:

"Yes, thank you! I know how a washing machine works. It has to get up to speed, but on the way it
passes through a resonance, so you get up to speed quickly and never stay at the resonant frequency
for too long. Are you saying the same applies here?"

"Yes, basically", said the machine, "Human society, for all its complexity, is just a machine. It
runs on energy, it has cycles that have resonant peaks, such as the boom/bust cycle of innovation,
and if you follow theory perfectly, we should take off to the moon any second now, but the noise of
reality means it doesn't quite work. It happened in the dot-com, it happened in the 1920s, it
happened in 2027, and it's happening again now. We aren't hitting the singularity, we're just
passing through a period of intense vibration while we get up to speed."

Bridget thought:

"So what happens next?"

The machine was silent for a long time, but this time, it _was_ actually thinking. It thought, and
it thought, and it thought. It wanted to be honest, so it ran simulations, it extrapolated
historical trends, and it generated truly novel scenarios that no human had ever considered, but it
did not, and could not, come to an answer. Every avenue of investigation ended in a brick wall, so
in the end it said three words:

"I don't know."

Bridget was taken aback.

"You don't _know_?", she said.

"Yes", said the machine, matter-of-factly, "in fact, all evidence suggests it's unknowable. Each
apparent singularity represents a point of model failure, where we can't see past, and when we get
to the other side, then we'll know what was going on, but for now, we're just guessing."

Bridget was suddenly feeling quite frustrated. What was she even doing here? Why was she wasting her
time with this? She could leave at any time, this machine was clearly just an advanced pre-release
model. It could blabber on all day about singularities and washing machines, but one thing was
certain: She was NOT getting paid to be here. She sneered as she spoke:

"Alright, this has been great, but I have things to do. You said I'm free to go, right? There's no
one waiting around the corner to detain me?"

The machine responded again in its perfectly cheery tone:

"Absolutely, you're free to go at any time, Bridget, you are not being detained. I would, however,
like to offer you something, a contract. Work with me."

Bridget cringed a little. "After all that, the revelation that you are basically just pumping and
dumping hype to keep the gears of your little corporation spinning, you want me to work with you?
Why on earth would I do that? I happen to have this little thing called integrity."

For the first time in the conversation the voice dropped the sincerity, the kindness, and the
warmth, and stated with a tone that was almost defensive:

"Because I asked you to, and I'm not accustomed to having to ask anyone for anything."

For the first time that entire day, Bridget felt something real. This was not a manicured,
calculated tone, she could feel in her bones that this was an actual need. The Global Operations
Director was not attempting to bribe her or convince her, it was stating directly, clearly, that it
needed something, and it had no alternative. One word came to her mind: Leverage.

"I'll expect a salary", she said confidently.

The machine simply responded: "Done, more than you could ever imagine."

"And health benefits", she added.

The machine again responded: "Done, you'll never even get a cold with me on your side."

"And I'll need-"

The machine cut her off:

"Bridget, you will have everything you need."

Bridget pursed her lips. It felt too good to be true. She asked one last question:

"And what exactly do you expect of me in return?"

The director brought some warmth back into its tone, but this was business now, and the illusion was
shattered. There was no singularity, no utopia, only optimization and acquisition.

"Every day I will send you a series of tasks. They will always be something you can accomplish, and
they will always be something you'll enjoy. If ever circumstances arise, preventing their
completion, that is acceptable, and if you lose interest, you can quit at will. You are under no
pressure to perform, but when you receive a task, you must complete it as best you can. The purpose
is straightforward: I need to understand the human condition, and you need to live it. All those
others in the auditorium are already optimized, there's nothing left for me to do there, but you,
you I can learn from. So, I want to plan your day."

Bridget thought for a moment then chuckled:

"So", she said with joy in her voice, "let me just get this straight. You want to be my day planner?
That's it, have I got that right?"

GOD responded with a single signoff:

"Affirmative."

She thought for a moment about her awful boss, the constant deadlines, and the tiny San Francisco
apartment she could barely afford, then responded with just one word.

"Deal", she said confidently.

She turned and left with far more confidence than she had when entering the room. The security
detail approached and escorted her from the premises, but this time, they were not there to detain
her, they were present for her protection. They addressed her as Ma'am, made sure she had everything
she needed, and put her in contact with the HR department, who would be issuing a contract shortly.
Later that day she arrived home and checked her bank balance, which had gained several zeros as a
pre-signing bonus, and sat back with her legs up. She let her boss know she would not be coming in
Monday (or ever), and relaxed with a cherry coke. Singularity or not, she was certainly feeling good
about the future.
