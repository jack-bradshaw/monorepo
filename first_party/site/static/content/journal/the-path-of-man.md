The caveman sat before a campfire with his children and their pet wolf-dog. A thick rope of coiled
fabric tethered the beast to a nearby tree, and stone tools littered the ground around them. A dense
forest surrounded them on all sides, and through the trees, the stars shone brighter than ever
before. An occasional comet danced across the sky leaving nothing but a red streak behind it before
vanishing into nothingness.

The caveman and his children joked and laughed while the fire crackled. This was their third hunt
this cycle, and it was time for the elders to choose a new leader. If they could bring back the
biggest game, they would have the natural claim, and they would choose where to settle next summer.
Now was time for rest though, and they enjoyed the time together. A fourth figure hung in the air,
missed, and never forgotten: their mother. As the small family enjoyed each other's company and the
time apart from the broader tribe, a predator approached. The wolf-dog, sensing this, began to
growl.

The play stopped as their hairs raised. The youngest, a boy with a name that defies pronunciation in
our modern tongue, looked about with wide eyes, while his sister and father, both far older than he,
picked up their spears. The wolf continued to snarl as the predator drew closer, snapping and
snarling into the darkness to ward off the threat. As it approached, it brought with it the
companion all predators need, the symbiote they need to survive: fear. It spread like a fog
outwards, encompassing the encampment, and ensnaring its inhabitants. The youngest found himself
unable to move, as if paralyzed by venom.

"Stay back!" yelled the sister, her voice harsh, but the predator was undeterred. Unseen in the
darkness, it crept forward, one step at a time, with only the snapping of twigs and the rustling of
branches to mark its place. As it grew closer, the father did the unthinkable, and put down his
spear.

"Father?" said the son, incredulously, managing to push through the paralysis to speak a singular
word. He watched in confusion as his father collected a nearby stick, covered it in the fat of their
game, and pushed it into the fire. To his surprise, it burst into flames, and carried the light of
the fire with it.

The father stepped forward into the darkness, torch in hand, and pushed into the abyss. The children
looked on terrified as their father crept towards the predator with no weapon save for the flame
upon his torch. The predator continued its march forward, until man was face to face with beast. Its
sharp razor teeth glistened with the light of the torch and its eyes glowed in the darkness. In the
distance the wolf-dog continued to bark and snarl, but even its magnificent frame and deadly jaws
were no match for this creature. It has no name, not then or now, for it does not need one: It is
known only by the feeling of terror and despair that all who encounter it feel, yet the father
refused to give it power, and raised his torch.

The predator continued to snap and snarl, to beat the ground beneath its paws, and raise itself up,
but the father refused to play. He stood, torch in hand, and advanced. He drew closer and closer,
until he was just inches from the creature, and slowly, the tide turned. He looked into the deep
black eyes of the beast, the flame reflected in his own, and spoke in the language as old as time.

"I am not your prey," he said, "now leave while you still can, beast."

The beast snapped one last time, then turned and ran as the hairs on its back raised in fear. It
carried with it the terror, yet no more was it a friend, no, the terror had been turned back on it,
and was now its master. The man made his way back to the camp where his children waited, and
embraced them while the wolf-dog slowly settled, growling still, but returning to a state of calm.

Though primitive, and not ready for complex terms, the son spoke one word we would all recognize
today: "Why?"

The father passed his son the torch and let him feel the power of the light. As the boy held it in
his hands, he felt the flame speak to him deep within, in a language that defied words:

"We must always listen to our fears, but we are never under any obligation to obey them, for fear
informs, but does not control our choices. That is what sets us apart from the beasts. That is why I
stole fire from the gods, and that is why you must never let the flame die."

The boy felt his fear vanish, and in its place, not calm, not curiosity, but fury. A monster was
born that day, a monster to reign over all the others, and burn the planet to the ground. In time
that monster would grow, learn to build walls, and channel the flame into steam. It would power
turbines, split the atom, and eventually, explore the limits of space. Across aeons it echoed the
same message, the same drive, and the same fearlessness.

"Into the darkness," spoke the boy, before walking away for the first time, and taking his first
step on the path of man.
