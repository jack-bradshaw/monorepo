The irritated philosopher paced frantically in his study. Oak bookshelves lined the walls, all
poorly organised, but each holding volumes of great knowledge, and despite the chaos, there was a
certain beauty to the disorder. As he paced the study, searching for a solution to all of life's
ills, a single thought stuck out like a sore thumb: What are we going to do about the righteous? Was
it not righteous certainty that caused all suffering, and how might the world be different if the
unenlightened masses knew how little they knew? He stopped pacing, sat at his large wooden desk, and
began writing with an old feather quill:

"Righteousness... what a terrible trait. Has there ever been a great untruth or a woeful tragedy
that was not in some way rooted in righteousness? I argue that righteousness is the fundamental
cause of all immoral and undesirable outcomes, and that humanity would do well to expunge it."

He paused as a subtle irony became clear, before continuing:

"We should probably take a moment to address a rather important truth: I am writing this article
from a place of conviction. In some ways, my own righteousness is present here. I see others holding
their truths over others', and it frustrates me because there are consequences that are in my view,
not worth it. I don’t believe it benefits society when individuals prostrate and punish others for
thinking differently. It is wrong in my view, but is this not the very righteousness we began with?
Hypocrisy!"

He paused, and thought, while a voice in the back of his mind asked "Where is this all going?", but
he ignored it and pushed on:

"In some ways yes. I hold my convictions, but righteousness is not black or white. We cannot go
about our lives believing in nothing, we simply couldn’t function that way. If the very ground
beneath our feet is unstable, we perish. It would seem the trick is learning to hold our
convictions, but let go of the righteous anger that leads to violence. In essence, we must be open
to the idea that our sacred views are not everyone’s sacred views, and make space for disagreement.
Not active argument, we don’t want to spend every moment fighting over truths, but passive
discordance. I’m trying to say that moving beyond righteousness doesn’t require changing our values,
it requires letting other people have theirs and develop at their own pace. The key here, is
tolerance, even for the intolerant."

There was a knock at the door and his colleague stepped inside. A bright young man in a worn brown
suit smiled and asked politely "Bertrand, are you coming to lunch?", but the irritated philosopher
simply waved him away without a look. The man pursed his lips in mild pity and left to enjoy his
luncheon, while the irritated philosopher continued to write:

"Let’s elaborate. We all begin somewhere, often before we have the skills or awareness to challenge
information and consider alternative viewpoints. We learn from our surroundings, and we take in what
other people tell us. It’s an unfortunate reality that we are all dependent on the views of others
at some point, and those views are often deeply ingrained. If we don’t choose our starting points,
we can’t be held responsible for it. At some point we gain the ability to challenge ourselves and
move beyond our inherited beliefs, and when that time comes, it’s far easier to change without
others shaming you for your inherited views."

He paused again while ink dripped from his quill and left stains on the page. A voice once again
asked him from the back of his mind: "Where is this all headed?", but he dismissed the voice and
continued to write:

"I believe this speaks to the fundamental problem with righteousness. It doesn’t help anyone grow,
it only makes it harder for other people to be self-compassionate and deal with whatever in their
life is causing them to hold views that are misaligned with reality. If you need proof, ask
yourself, how do you feel when other people belittle you or put you down, simply because you dared
to say something on your mind? Did it help you work through it, and did you find yourself
considering new possibilities, or were you driven further into the idea?"

He looked over his last sentence and a crooked smile appeared. His philosophical might and command
of language truly were remarkable, he thought to himself, before continuing:

"To summarise, righteousness comes from holding convictions so deeply that we begin to view other
people’s ideas as not just incorrect, but shameful. It makes it harder to see others compassionately
and recognise their inherent personhood, and when this results in some people being raised above
others in terms of worth, it drives the despicable views of others further into conviction. Not
exactly a great long term plan for society now, is it?"

He paused again, stood, and began pacing the room, wondering where to go next in his essay. He
opened the window to let in a breeze, before returning to his desk to write. The curtains billowed
slightly as his pen once again marked the page:

"So what are we to do? Well, let’s consider the case in point for a moment and examine those of
righteous conviction through this new perspective. If we look at them compassionately, we once again
see people who are struggling with something. For whatever reason, and it will differ based on who
you ask, they are struggling to integrate their humanity, and they have not yet accepted that we are
all flawed and often hold deeply inaccurate views of the world. It’s part of being human to be
wrong, and it’s part of being human to want to be right. Let’s not be righteous about righteousness,
let's acknowledge it for what it is: human."

His smile returned. "Brilliant", he thought to himself, "absolutely brilliant". He had in one
sitting dissected the core of the world's issues, all the while avoiding the paradoxical trap of
becoming caught up in his own righteousness. He sealed the document in an envelope, addressed it to
his rival in Europe (convinced that surely this time he would emerge the intellectual superior in
their endless battle of wits), but just as he was about to seal it, he was distracted by a sound
from afar. A laugh carried through the window, and as his attention slipped, so did his finger,
leaving him with a deep papercut and blood stains across his letter. He cursed and sucked his thumb,
before turning to the window to discover the source of the commotion. He surveyed the scene with a
stern look until the source was found.

Beyond the walls of the disorganised study, children played in the nearby park. A child with red
hair and freckles threw a ball to another, and an elderly woman, their chaperone, watched from a
bench. Various others joined, and as they played, the sound of laughter floated through the
neighbourhood. None had convictions beyond the basics needed to survive childhood, and none were
particularly attached to their beliefs. If the irritated philosopher had simply considered the day
from their perspective, perhaps he would no longer need to question conviction and righteousness so
deeply, but sadly, it was not to be.

He ignored the distraction and returned to his work, but as the noise grated against his eardrums,
he found himself unable to think. He paced, and paced, and paced, searching for a way to manifest
his thoughts and free the world of the stranglehold of conviction, but as his letter sat upon the
table, the noise was all he could focus on. The children's laughter floated in through the open
window, and reminded him of a distant memory from decades prior, one where questions of conviction
and righteousness were the least of his worries. He smiled, but it lasted but a moment, before the
stern philosopher returned. In one swift motion he stood, slammed the window shut, and returned to
the present. "There is work to be done", he reminded himself, before returning to the endless
torture of thought.
