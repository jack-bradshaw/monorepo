The panel of economists, financiers, and industrialists sat together in private to discuss matters
of great importance.

"We must ensure interest rates remain steady!" claimed one, "it is the only way to survive in an
environment of fiscal dominance!"

There was a great uproar from the table as the group erupted.

"NO!" claimed another, "We must find more resources and innovate. The only way to stimulate the
economy is to increase the production of goods and eliminate wasteful practices. Innovation must
remain supreme."

This continued for several minutes, and in all that time, despite all the commotion and all the
discussion, there was only one person listening. She sat in the corner, legs crossed, lips pursed,
eyes sharp. She knew that interjecting now was an inefficient use of resources, so like the good
economist she was, she waited. In time, the rabble died down, and the group came to a consensus. A
man stood, old and frail, with glasses that refused to remain on the bridge of his nose. He spoke
with conviction:

"Well now, well now, what a day. Thank goodness we got that sorted out. Since we're all agreed,
we'll keep interest rates steady, adjust policy to innovate in tech and life sciences, and
disincentivize exorbitant spending with cuts to consumer incentives. All agreed."

There was a resounding "aye" from the room, except the lone economist, who raised her hand.

"Yessss, Madame?" said the elderly speaker.

She waited for a moment and chewed on her words before choosing to simply spit it out.

"We've been over this time and time again but it has to be said again. You're all reasoning as if
human beings are sensible people with reasonable goals, but have you ever actually met a human
being? I have, quite a few in fact, and I have to tell you, human beings are the furthest thing from
reasonable, rational, sensible actors there are. The average human believes in magic, has no clue
how the economy works, and thinks that a savings account is a retirement strategy. Human beings, and
humanity as a whole, are morons. Your changes are going to fail and we're going to be right back
here at square one, trying desperately to fix the economy, because the whole premise, that people
are rational, is wrong, and is ironically, one of the most irrational foundations anyone has ever
laid."

The room was silent for a few moments, save for a cleared throat and an uncomfortable sigh, before
the elderly speaker continued:

"Yes... well thank you Madame, we are all aware of your views on the economy, and people. Your
comments are noted, but I believe we have reached majority consensus, so moving on. Next order of
business: The coffee in the break room has been running low lately. Mr. Johans, you were tasked with
restocking it, what do you have to say for yourself, is it a matter of supply, or demand?"

The economist rolled her eyes. As usual she would not be heard, and as usual the policies of the
central committee would fail to achieve the desired effect, doing little but further inflating the
economy roughly at the same rate as the already rather voluptuous egos filling the room. While the
others continued to discuss matters of fiscal prudence, she wondered, what on earth was the point of
all this? Why was she here? What on earth was to gain from talking to these men all day about math
when the rest of the species was running on fumes and feelings?

Nothing. There was no reason to be here.

"Ironic," she thought, "I understand this system perfectly and yet it applies equally to me."

She stood to leave, giving the others the space to hash out their irrelevant minutiae, knowing that
it would all come crashing down once the world ran out of caffeine. After a brief detour to fetch a
cup of tea from her office, she made her way to the exit and headed to the nearby marketplace. The
solid beige concrete of the central chamber loomed behind her, imposing and powerful, yet clearly
bankrupt in all the ways that mattered. She put it out of her mind and focused on the task at hand.
It was a tradition of hers to browse the nearby shops on occasion as a reminder of how people
actually live. So much of economics is abstract, she thought, but in the end, what is economics if
not a system for setting the price tag? She casually strolled through the street, passing overpriced
jewelers and hyperinflated technology distributors, until an antique store caught her eye. Antiques
were a favorite anomaly of hers, for what were obsolete possessions if not the purest example of
human irrationality?

She slowly made her way through the store. Wooden shelves lined every inch, each littered with
trinkets and paperweights. The store did not sell dust, but it was certainly taking up considerable
floor space, and should probably be on the balance sheet. The economist browsed until she found a
small book, a translation of the Tao Te Ching by Stephen Mitchell. She began to read, and after a
few pages, found herself deeply immersed in it. For the first time in many years, gone were the
thoughts of macroeconomics and monetary supply, and in its place: A sense that had eluded her since
childhood. She purchased the book for a mere $2 USD plus tax, exited the store, and came to rest on
a nearby park bench.

She pored over its pages in the shade of a nearby tree, and read its entirety in just 25 minutes,
and as she closed the final page, the words sunk in. Her mind traced the meaning, the messages, and
the references between passages. She found the balance between the words, let the poetry speak for
itself, and noticed a deep pit inside her steadily losing its weight, as if a debt were being
repaid. Slowly, but surely, peace emerged from deep within her. How could it be that such simple
words, such cheap words, could touch so deeply, she wondered, but no answer came to her.

She reflected for a moment, before pulling a pen from her pocket and scribbling on the only paper
she had left: The back of the book. As her pen streamed across the page with the ease of an ice
skater, the words poured from the space in her heart that had laid dormant for so long. It read:

"We are not free, yet there are no controls.\
We have no self, and there are no others.\
We have no end, for there are no beginnings.

Such arbitrary distinctions are distractions.\
Artifacts of a mind grasping for answers.

We search for structure, and so we find barriers.\
We search for answers, and so we find questions.\
We search for meaning, and so we find nonsense.

In grasping, we find nothing beyond our understanding.

From that nothingness arises reality.\
Reality beyond comprehension.\
From that confusion arises chaos.\
Chaos within chaos.\
From that chaos arises order.\
Order beyond sense.

An unending rhythm.\
An eternal puzzle.\
An answer that begs a question.

The mind holds the lock.\
Always prying it open.

The heart holds the key.\
Waiting to be acknowledged.

The space between them holds the truth.\
Waiting for release.

All three exactly where they need to be.

Let go of the need for understanding, and the truth will flourish.\
Let go of the need to hear, and the universe will sing.

That song is all we really need.

Can you hear it?"

Her words felt paltry compared to the wisdom she clung to, but they were not written to know or to
speak, and they were not written with intention. They came from a voice that had laid dormant for so
long and had finally remembered how to speak. As the voice became a stream, it carved its way
through decades of calcification and fossilization, until it reached her eyes and tears began to
flow. As decades of striving came to a halt, a peaceful stillness emerged, until all that remained
was the sound of the birds, the cool breeze through her hair, and the gentle rise and fall of her
chest. In time a mild hunger emerged, breaking her tranquil repose, and beckoning her to leave, to
return to the rat race, and continue on her way.

She sat with the feeling for some time until a woman approached from the nearby path. Her dark skin
and gentle eyes betrayed a wisdom and kindness years alone could not buy, and she held herself with
the grace of one who had learned to see thousands of years as no more than a heartbeat.

"Peaceful, here, isn't it?" she said with a smile, before offering a small red treat.

"Candy?" she asked.

The economist gratefully took the sweet, but found herself drawn to ask:

"How much?"

The old woman continued to smile and simply said:

"Did you not just read? The sage does not hoard. The more he does for others, the more he has. The
more he thereby gives to others, the ever more he gets."

The economist looked into the eyes of the woman with curiosity, but for the first time, she did not
see another. She did not see a stranger, nor an other, nor an ego of any kind. All she saw was a
reflection, and like a child finally coming to understand a mirror, she recognized herself. She
blinked, and in an instant, the woman was gone, the park was nowhere, and the book was nowhere to be
found.

She woke suddenly from her daydream, and all that surrounded her were ledgers and spreadsheets.
Digits danced across the page in columns, tracking balances and allocating what to who, with red
marking those in dire need of refinancing. She looked out the nearby window of her small, gloomy
office, and wondered how long had passed while she was elsewhere. It could not have been more than a
few minutes, for her tea was still warm, but it felt like a lifetime. Her body felt lighter and well
rested, as if she had just slept for days.

She sat and reflected on the experience, no longer interested in the balance sheets and documents
strewn before her, and an answer finally appeared:

Yes, she could hear it, but more importantly, there was nothing to hear.
